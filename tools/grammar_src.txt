@@ how-to-study | 🧭 | start | How to learn Lebanese with Kifak
> A plan for your first three months, and how to use each part of the site.
## The short version
Listen first, read second. Lebanese is a spoken language: people rarely write it except in text messages, so your ear matters more than your eyes.
Do a little every day. Fifteen minutes daily beats two hours on Sunday.
Say everything out loud. The **Repeat** step in each lesson records you so you can compare with the native voice.
## A plan for the first three months
| Weeks | What to do | Goal
| 1 to 2 | Reading Latin-script Arabic, then Level 1 | Read 2, 3, 7, kh, gh, sh without thinking. 300 words.
| 3 to 4 | Level 2, the Grammar basics, Review every day | Put two or three words together.
| 5 to 8 | Levels 3 and 4, Verbs, Conversations of Level 1 to 4 | Ask and answer simple questions.
| 9 to 12 | Levels 5 and 6, the Culture pages | Talk about your day and what you did yesterday.
## How to use each part
**Lessons:** the main path. Learn the cards, repeat them, then take the quiz. Each quiz picks 15 new examples, so take it more than once.
**Review:** flashcards of words from lessons you finished. Cards you know come back less often; cards you miss come back soon. Do it every day.
**Conversations:** real dialogues. Listen once without reading, then with the text, then answer the questions.
**Verbs:** the tables. Learn the "every day" form first, because the others are built from it.
**Grammar** and **Culture:** read a page when a lesson raises a question.
**Dictionary:** search any word in English, Latin letters or Arabic.
tip: Hide the English with the EN button once a lesson feels easy. Hide the Latin letters too if you're learning the Arabic script.
## What to expect
Lebanese people switch between Arabic, French and English, often in one sentence. You'll hear "merci", "bonjour" and "ok" all day. That's normal Lebanese, not a mistake.
Most Lebanese will answer you in English or French the moment they hear an accent. Smile and keep going in Arabic: "baddi et3allam 3arabe" (I want to learn Arabic). Most people love it.
ex: baddi et3allam lebnene | بدّي اتعلّم لبناني | I want to learn Lebanese
ex: 7ke shway shway, iza bitred | احكي شوي شوي، إذا بتريد | Speak slowly, please | to a man; "iza btride" to a woman
ex: shu ya3ne ...? | شو يعني ...؟ | What does ... mean?
ex: kif bt2oul ... bil lebnene? | كيف بتقول ... باللبناني؟ | How do you say ... in Lebanese?
ex: 3id marra please | عيد مرّة please | Say it again, please
ex: ma fhemt | ما فهمت | I didn't understand

@@ sounds | 👄 | start | The sounds of Lebanese
> The letters English doesn't have, doubled letters, and which syllable to stress.
## Sounds English doesn't have
| Latin | Arabic | How to say it | Example
| 2 | ء | A tiny stop in the throat, like the break in "uh-oh". | su2al (question)
| 3 | ع | Squeeze the throat, as if starting to gag, then say "a". | 3afwan (you're welcome)
| 7 | ح | A strong breathy "h", like fogging up glasses. | mni7 (good)
| kh | خ | Like the "ch" in Scottish "loch". | kheir (good)
| gh | غ | A gargled "r", like a French "r". | ghali (expensive)
| sh | ش | "sh" as in "shop". | shukran (thanks)
## Heavy letters
Four letters have a "heavy" twin, said with the tongue pulled back: ص (s), ض (d), ط (t), ظ (z). Latin-script Arabic writes them like the light ones, so "sayf" and "seif" can look alike. The heavy ones make the vowel near them sound darker, more like "o" or "a" in "father".
tip: You don't need the heavy sounds to be understood at first. Get 3, 7 and 2 right first; people will understand you.
## Doubled letters matter
A doubled letter is held longer, and it changes the meaning. Listen carefully to these pairs.
ex: 7amam | حمام | pigeons | single m
ex: 7ammam | حمّام | bathroom | double m: hold it
ex: 3amal | عمل | work (noun)
ex: 3ammal | عمّال | workers
## The vowels
Lebanese has short a, e, i, o, u and long ones. In Latin letters people write "e" for a sound between "e" in "bed" and "a" in "cat". Long vowels are often written double or with "ei" and "ou": "beit" (house), "rou7" (go).
In Beirut many "a" sounds at the end of words become "e": "a7la" in a book is "a7le" in the street. That's the "imala", and it's one of the clearest marks of Lebanese.
ex: shu 3amle? | شو عاملة؟ | How are you doing? (to a woman) | the -a ending sounds like -e
ex: a7la beit | أحلى بيت | the nicest house
## Stress
Stress the long vowel if there is one: "sha-BEB" (young men), "ma-TAR" (airport). If there isn't, stress the second-to-last syllable: "MAD-rase" (school). Lebanese is spoken fast, and short vowels often disappear: "ktir", not "katir".
!: Saying "h" for 7 or nothing for 3. "3am" (in the middle of) and "am" don't mean the same thing.
!: Rushing doubled letters. "7ammam" (bathroom) said with one m is "7amam" (pigeons).
?: Which number stands for the letter ع? | 2 | *3 | 7
?: In "7ammam" (bathroom), what do you do with the m? | *hold it longer | skip it | say it as n
?: Which sound is like the "ch" in Scottish "loch"? | gh | *kh | sh

@@ pronouns | 👤 | basics | I, you, he, she: pronouns
> The eight people every verb and every ending is built on.
## The eight pronouns
| Latin | Arabic | English
| ana | أنا | I
| enta | إنت | you (to a man)
| ente | إنتِ | you (to a woman)
| huwwe | هوّي | he, it
| hiyye | هيّي | she, it
| ne7na | نحنا | we
| ento | إنتو | you (to more than one)
| hinne | هنّي | they
Lebanese has three ways to say "you": one for a man, one for a woman, one for a group. Every verb changes with them, so learn which one you are using.
English "it" is huwwe or hiyye: every noun is masculine or feminine (see the next topic).
## You often drop them
The verb already shows who is talking, so the pronoun is extra. Use it for emphasis or contrast.
ex: ana lebnene, w enta? | أنا لبناني، وإنت؟ | I'm Lebanese, and you?
ex: hiyye bta3ref, ana la2 | هيّي بتعرف، أنا لأ | She knows, I don't
ex: ne7na men beirut | نحنا من بيروت | We're from Beirut
ex: hinne hon | هنّي هون | They're here
ex: ento kifkon? | إنتو كيفكن؟ | How are you all?
tip: Lebanese use "ento" (plural) to be polite to one older person, a bit like French "vous". "kifkon?" to your friend's grandmother sounds warm.

?: Which one means "we"? | hinne | *ne7na | huwwe
?: "it" for a feminine thing is… | huwwe | *hiyye | hinne

@@ to-be | 🟰 | basics | "To be": I am, I was, I'll be
> There's no "am / is / are" in the present. You just put the words side by side.
## Present: no verb at all
"I'm tired" is simply "I tired". "The house is big" is "the house big".
ex: ana te3ban | أنا تعبان | I'm tired | a woman says "ana te3bene"
ex: el beit kbir | البيت كبير | The house is big
ex: huwwe doctor | هوّي دكتور | He's a doctor
ex: wein enta? | وين إنت؟ | Where are you?
## Negative: mesh
Put **mesh** before the word: "mesh te3ban" (not tired). Some people say "mish" or "ma-ni" forms ("mani te3ban"); "mesh" works everywhere.
ex: ana mesh te3ban | أنا مش تعبان | I'm not tired
ex: el akel mesh ghali | الأكل مش غالي | The food isn't expensive
## Past: ken
For the past you need a verb: ken (was).
| Latin | Arabic | English
| ana kent | أنا كنت | I was
| enta kent | إنت كنت | you were (to a man)
| ente kente | إنتِ كنتي | you were (to a woman)
| huwwe ken | هوّي كان | he was
| hiyye kenet | هيّي كانت | she was
| ne7na kenna | نحنا كنّا | we were
| ento kento | إنتو كنتو | you were (group)
| hinne keno | هنّي كانو | they were
ex: kent bel beit | كنت بالبيت | I was at home
ex: ken fi 3aj2a | كان في عجقة | There was traffic
ex: ma kent hon | ما كنت هون | I wasn't here | past negative uses "ma"
## Future: ra7 koun
ex: ra7 koun hon bukra | رح كون هون بكرا | I'll be here tomorrow
ex: ra7 ykoun mabsout | رح يكون مبسوط | He'll be happy
## "ken" + verb: used to, was doing
Put ken before an every-day verb (without b-) and you get "used to". Put it before 3am and you get "was doing".
ex: kent rou7 3al ba7r kel yom | كنت روح عالبحر كل يوم | I used to go to the sea every day
ex: kent 3am ekol lamma 2ejet | كنت عم اكل لمّا إجت | I was eating when she came
!: Adding a word for "is": "el beit huwwe kbir" sounds odd. Just "el beit kbir".
!: Using "ma" in the present: it's "mesh te3ban", not "ma te3ban".
?: "The food is good" is… | el akel ken tayyeb | *el akel tayyeb | el akel 3am tayyeb
?: "I'm not hungry": | ma ana jou3an | *ana mesh jou3an | ana jou3an la2
?: "I was at work": | *kent bel shoghol | ra7 koun bel shoghol | ana bel shoghol

@@ gender | ⚧ | basics | Masculine and feminine
> Every noun is a he or a she, and the words around it follow.
## How to spot a feminine word
Most feminine words end in **-e** or **-a** (in Arabic, ة): sayyara (car), madrase (school), tawle (table), shante (bag).
Some are feminine without the ending: words for women (emm: mother), most body parts that come in pairs (iid: hand, 3ein: eye), and some others like ard (ground) and shams (sun).
## Things that follow the gender
Adjectives, "this", verbs and endings all change.
| | Masculine | Feminine
| big | kbir | kbire
| new | jdid | jdide
| this | hayda | hayde
| he/she went | ra7 | ra7et
| my… | ktebe (my book) | sayyarte (my car)
ex: beit kbir | بيت كبير | a big house
ex: sayyara kbire | سيّارة كبيرة | a big car
ex: hayda l kteb jdid | هيدا الكتاب جديد | This book is new
ex: hayde l shante jdide | هيدي الشنتة جديدة | This bag is new
## Talking to a man or a woman
Lebanese also changes words depending on who you talk to. "How are you?" is "kifak?" to a man and "kifik?" to a woman. Use the "Talking to" switch in lessons to see both.
ex: kifak? | كيفك؟ | How are you? (to a man)
ex: kifik? | كيفِك؟ | How are you? (to a woman)
ex: tfaddal | تفضّل | Go ahead / please (to a man)
ex: tfaddale | تفضّلي | Go ahead / please (to a woman)
## Talking about yourself
A woman says "ana te3bene", "ana mabsouta", "ana lebnenye". A man says "ana te3ban", "ana mabsout", "ana lebnene". Get this right about yourself first.
!: Using the man's form for yourself if you're a woman (or the other way). People will understand, but it's the mistake they notice most.
?: Which word is feminine? | beit | *madrase | kteb
?: "a new car": | sayyara jdid | *sayyara jdide | jdide sayyara
?: A woman says "I'm happy": | ana mabsout | *ana mabsouta | ana mabsoutin

@@ plurals | 👥 | basics | Plurals and "two of"
> -in, -et, the "broken" plurals, and the -ein ending for pairs.
## Regular plurals
Most words for people and jobs add **-in**: m3allem → m3allmin (teachers), lebnene → lebnenyin (Lebanese people).
Most feminine words swap -e/-a for **-et**: sayyara → sayyarat (cars), tawle → tawlet (tables), shante → shanatet (bags).
## Broken plurals
Many common words change their inside instead. There's a pattern, but learn them one by one.
| Latin one | Latin many | English
| beit | byout | house, houses
| walad | wled | child, children
| kteb | kotob | book, books
| shaghle | shoghol | thing, things | also "ghrad"
| shabb | shabeb | young man, young men
| balad | blad | country, countries
| sa7eb | s7ab | friend, friends
| sh2a | sho2a2 | flat, flats
ex: 3ande tlet wled | عندي تلت ولاد | I have three children
ex: el byout ghalye | البيوت غالية | Houses are expensive
## Two of something: -ein
Add **-ein** for exactly two: yom → yomein (two days), sene → sentein (two years), kilo → kiloyein. You don't need the number 2.
ex: yomein | يومين | two days
ex: sentein | سنتين | two years
ex: kiloyein banadoura | كيلوين بندورة | two kilos of tomatoes
## Things in the plural are "she"
For plural things (not people), adjectives often take the feminine: "el byout ghalye", "sayyarat jdide". People stay plural: "el shabeb mabsoutin".
!: Saying "tnein yom" for two days. Lebanese say "yomein".

?: Plural of "sayyara" (car): | sayyarin | *sayyarat | sayayir

@@ the | 🔠 | basics | "The": el- and the sun letters
> One word for "the", and why it sometimes sounds like ech-, et- or es-.
## el- means "the"
Put **el** (or just **l** after a vowel) in front: beit (a house), el beit (the house). There's no word for "a": beit is "a house".
ex: beit | بيت | a house
ex: el beit | البيت | the house
ex: fi kteb 3al tawle | في كتاب عالطاولة | There's a book on the table | "3al" = 3a + el
## Sun letters
Before t, d, s, sh, z, r, n, l and the heavy letters, the l of "el" melts into the next letter. You write "el shams" but say "esh-shams". The Latin spelling on this site follows what you hear in some places and what you write in others, so listen to the recording.
| Written | Said | English
| el shams | esh shams | the sun
| el se3a | es se3a | the time, the hour
| el ta2s | et ta2s | the weather
| el nes | en nes | the people
| el 2amar | el 2amar | the moon | not a sun letter: l is said
ex: esh shams 2awiyye lyom | الشمس قويّة اليوم | The sun is strong today
ex: en nes ktir latifin | الناس كتير لطيفين | People are very nice
## When to use el-
Lebanese uses "the" more than English: for things in general ("I love coffee" = "b7ebb el ahwe"), for languages, and for names of meals.
ex: b7ebb el ahwe | بحبّ القهوة | I love coffee
tip: "the" plus a preposition usually shrinks: "3a + el beit" → "3al beit" (to the house), "b + el beit" → "bel beit" (at home), "la + el beit" → "lal beit".
?: "the house": | beit el | *el beit | hal beit
?: "I love coffee": | b7ebb ahwe | *b7ebb el ahwe | ana ahwe
?: "at home": | *bel beit | b beit | 3and beit

@@ possessives | 🔑 | basics | My, your, his: the endings
> Lebanese doesn't say "my book", it says "book-my". Learn the eight endings once and they work everywhere.
## The endings
| Ending | Example | English
| -e | ktebe | my book
| -ak | ktebak | your book (man)
| -ik | ktebik | your book (woman)
| -o | ktebo | his book
| -a | kteba | her book
| -na | ktebna | our book
| -kon | ktebkon | your book (group)
| -on | ktebon | their book
## Feminine words get a t
A word ending in -e/-a gets a **t** before the ending: sayyara → sayyarte (my car), sayyartak (your car), sayyarta (her car).
ex: sayyarte bara | سيّارتي برّا | My car is outside
ex: shu ismak? | شو اسمك؟ | What's your name? (to a man)
ex: shu ismik? | شو اسمِك؟ | What's your name? (to a woman)
ex: emmo men 3akkar | إمّو من عكّار | His mother is from Akkar
ex: beitna 2rib | بيتنا قريب | Our house is near
## The same endings everywhere
These endings also go on prepositions ("ma3e": with me, "3ande": at me = I have) and on verbs as "me, you, him" ("shefto": I saw him, "b7ebbik": I love you, to a woman). Learn them here and you've learned them for the whole language.
## The other way: taba3
You can also say "el kteb taba3e" (the book of mine). It's useful for emphasis and for long or foreign words: "el laptop taba3e" sounds more natural than "laptope".
ex: el laptop taba3e | اللابتوب تبعي | my laptop
ex: hayda taba3ak? | هيدا تبعك؟ | Is this yours?
?: "my car": | sayyarae | *sayyarte | sayyara-e
?: "her name": | ismo | *isma | ismik
?: "Is this yours?" (to a man): | hayda taba3o? | *hayda taba3ak? | hayda taba3e?

@@ this-that | 👉 | basics | This and that
> hayda, hayde, hawde, and the short "hal".
## The words
| Latin | Arabic | English
| hayda | هيدا | this (masculine)
| hayde | هيدي | this (feminine)
| hawde | هودي | these
| haydak | هيداك | that (masculine)
| haydik | هيديك | that (feminine)
| hawdik | هوديك | those
ex: hayda khayye | هيدا خيّي | This is my brother

## "this thing": hal
Before a noun, Lebanese often use the short **hal**: "hal beit" (this house), "hal marra" (this time). It's the same for men and women.
ex: hal beit 7elo | هالبيت حلو | This house is pretty
ex: hal marra bas | هالمرّة بس | Just this time
ex: hayda l kteb | هيدا الكتاب | this book | the long form: hayda + el

@@ adjectives | 🎨 | basics | Describing things, comparing
> The adjective goes after the noun. "Bigger" and "the biggest" are one short word.
## After the noun, matching it
ex: beit kbir | بيت كبير | a big house
ex: el beit el kbir | البيت الكبير | the big house | "the" on both words
ex: el beit kbir | البيت كبير | the house is big | "the" on the noun only makes a sentence
## Bigger, the biggest
Most adjectives have a short form for comparing: kbir → akbar (bigger), zghir → azghar, 7elo → a7la, ghali → aghla, rkhis → arkhas, ktir → aktar, mni7 → a7san. Add **men** for "than".
| Latin | Comparative | English
| kbir | akbar | bigger
| zghir | azghar | smaller
| 7elo | a7la | nicer, prettier
| ghali | aghla | more expensive
| sa3b | as3ab | harder
| 2rib | a2rab | nearer
| mni7 | a7san | better
ex: beirut akbar men jbeil | بيروت أكبر من جبيل | Beirut is bigger than Byblos
ex: hayda a7san | هيدا أحسن | This one is better
## The best, the biggest
Put the comparative first: "akbar beit" (the biggest house), "a7la wa7ad" (the nicest one).
ex: a7la akle bel 3alam | أحلى أكلة بالعالم | the best food in the world
ex: aghla wa7de | أغلى وحدة | the most expensive one (feminine)
## Long adjectives: aktar
Adjectives that don't have a short form use **aktar** (more): "mertee7 aktar" (more comfortable).
## ktir and shway
ktir = very, a lot (after or before the adjective). shway = a bit. "ktir ktir" = really very.
ex: ktir 7elo | كتير حلو | very nice
ex: shway ghali | شوي غالي | a bit expensive
!: Putting the adjective first: "kbir beit". It's "beit kbir".
?: "the big house": | el kbir beit | *el beit el kbir | beit el kbir
?: "better": | mni7 aktar | *a7san | amna7
?: "Beirut is bigger than Byblos": | beirut kbir jbeil | *beirut akbar men jbeil | beirut akbar jbeil men

## Counting things: the short forms
Before a noun, 3 to 10 lose their ending: tlet (3), arba3 (4), khams (5), set (6), sab3 (7), tmen (8), tes3 (9), 3ashr (10). The noun is plural.
ex: tlet byout | تلت بيوت | three houses
ex: khams da2aye2 | خمس دقايق | five minutes
ex: 3ashr dollar | عشر دولار | ten dollars | money often stays singular
## One and two
"One" goes after the noun or is just the noun: "kteb" (a book), "kteb wa7ad" (one book). "Two" is the -ein ending: "ktebein".
## 11 and up
11 7da3sh, 12 tna3sh, 13 tletta3sh, 14 arba3ta3sh, 15 khamsta3sh, 16 setta3sh, 17 saba3ta3sh, 18 tmenta3sh, 19 tes3ta3sh. From 11 up, the noun goes back to singular: "tna3sh sene" (12 years).
Tens: 20 3eshrin, 30 tletin, 40 arb3in, 50 khamsin, 60 settin, 70 sab3in, 80 tmenin, 90 tes3in. 25 is "khamse w 3eshrin" (five and twenty).
| Latin | Number
| miyye | 100
| mitein | 200
| tlet miyye | 300
| alf | 1,000
| alfein | 2,000
| tlet alef | 3,000
| malyoun | 1,000,000
ex: khamse w 3eshrin sene | خمسة وعشرين سنة | 25 years
ex: miyye w khamsin alf | مية وخمسين ألف | 150,000
## Prices
Prices are said with the number first: "3ashr dollar", "khamsin alf". In daily life people often just say the number: "addeish? 3eshrin" (How much? Twenty).
ex: addeish 7a2o? | قدّيش حقّو؟ | How much is it?
ex: hayda b 3eshrin dollar | هيدا بعشرين دولار | This is twenty dollars
## First, second
awwal (first), tene (second), telet (third), rabe3 (fourth), akher (last). "awwal marra" = the first time.
ex: awwal marra b lebnen? | أوّل مرّة بلبنان؟ | First time in Lebanon?
ex: el tabe2 el tene | الطابق التاني | the second floor
!: "tlete byout": before a noun use the short form "tlet byout".
!: "tna3sh snin": after 10, the noun is singular again, "tna3sh sene".

## The cheat sheet

> One tiny word does the work of "there is", "there are", "is there?"
## fi
ex: fi farmashiyye hon? | في فرمشيّة هون؟ | Is there a pharmacy here?
ex: fi nes ktir | في ناس كتير | There are a lot of people
ex: ma fi mashkal | ما في مشكل | No problem (there's no problem)
ex: ma fi kahraba | ما في كهربا | There's no electricity
## Past and future
ex: ken fi 3aj2a | كان في عجقة | There was traffic

@@ want-can-must | 🎯 | sentences | Want, can, have to
> badde, fiye / 2ader, lezim, and the verb that follows them.
## badde: I want
badde takes the possessive endings, like 3ande.
| Latin | Arabic | English
| badde | بدّي | I want
| baddak | بدّك | you want (man)
| baddik | بدِّك | you want (woman)
| baddo | بدّو | he wants
| badda | بدّا | she wants
| badna | بدنا | we want
| badkon | بدكن | you want (group)
| badon | بدّن | they want
After badde, the verb drops its b-: "bekol" (I eat) → "badde ekol" (I want to eat).
ex: badde ahwe | بدّي قهوة | I want a coffee
ex: badde rou7 3al beit | بدّي روح عالبيت | I want to go home
ex: shu baddak tekol? | شو بدّك تاكل؟ | What do you want to eat? (to a man)
ex: ma badde | ما بدّي | I don't want to
## Can: fiye or 2ader
**fiye** (fiye, fik, fike, fi, fia, fina, fikon, fion) and **2ader** (I'm able; 2adra for a woman) both mean "can". fiye is more common for permission, 2ader for ability.
ex: fiye 2a3od hon? | فيي اقعد هون؟ | Can I sit here?
ex: fik tsa3edne? | فيك تساعدني؟ | Can you help me? (to a man)
ex: ma fiye | ما فيي | I can't
ex: mesh 2ader nem | مش قادر نام | I can't sleep
## Have to: lezim
**lezim** doesn't change. Put the verb (without b-) after it.
ex: lezim rou7 hala2 | لازم روح هلّق | I have to go now
ex: lezim tejarrib el knefe | لازم تجرّب الكنافة | You have to try the knafeh
ex: mesh lezim | مش لازم | You don't have to / no need
## Other words that work the same way
ma3 enno (although), 3ashen (so that), 2abel ma (before), ba3d ma (after): all followed by the verb without b-.
ex: 2abel ma tfell | قبل ما تفلّ | before you leave
!: "badde berou7": after badde, drop the b-: "badde rou7".
!: Changing lezim: it stays "lezim" for everyone.
?: "I want to eat": | badde bekol | *badde ekol | ana badde akel
?: "Can I sit here?": | *fiye 2a3od hon? | lezim 2a3od hon? | badde 2a3od hon?
?: "I have to go": | badde lezim rou7 | *lezim rou7 | lezim berou7

@@ time | ⏰ | sentences | Time: today, tomorrow, what time is it?
> Days, parts of the day, telling the time and how often.
## Days
| Latin | Arabic | English
| lyom | اليوم | today
| bukra | بكرا | tomorrow
| ba3d bukra | بعد بكرا | the day after tomorrow
| mberi7 | مبارح | yesterday
| awwal mberi7 | أوّل مبارح | the day before yesterday
| hala2 | هلّق | now
| ba3dein | بعدين | later
| ba3d shway | بعد شوي | in a little while

## Parts of the day
sob7 / el sob7 (morning), ba3d el dohr (afternoon), el masa (evening), el leil (night), el dohr (noon). "el sob7" + "bukra" = "bukra el sob7" (tomorrow morning).
ex: bukra el sob7 | بكرا الصبح | tomorrow morning
ex: mberi7 bel leil | مبارح بالليل | last night
## What time is it?
"addeish el se3a?" Answer: el se3a + number. Add w (and) for minutes past, illa (minus) for minutes to. robe3 = quarter, nos = half, tilt = twenty minutes.
ex: addeish el se3a? | قدّيش الساعة؟ | What time is it?
ex: el se3a tlete | الساعة تلاتة | It's three o'clock
ex: el se3a tlete w nos | الساعة تلاتة ونصّ | It's half past three
ex: el se3a arba3a illa robe3 | الساعة أربعة إلّا ربع | It's quarter to four
ex: el se3a sab3a w tilt | الساعة سبعة وتلت | It's twenty past seven
## How often
kel yom (every day), kel jem3a (every week), marra bel jem3a (once a week), dayman (always), ma3 el wa2t (with time), ba3d (still/yet), ba3d ma (not yet: "ba3d ma akalt" = I haven't eaten yet).
ex: ba3d ma akalt | بعد ما أكلت | I haven't eaten yet
ex: marra bel jem3a | مرّة بالجمعة | once a week | "jem3a" is both Friday and week
## Lebanese time
"hala2" (now) can mean in five minutes or in an hour. "nshalla" on a plan means "I hope so", not "yes". Ask "akid?" (sure?) if you need a promise.

@@ verb-system | ⚙️ | verbs | How Lebanese verbs work
> One table that explains every tense on the Verbs pages.
## Two stems, five uses
Every verb has a **past** form and a **present** form. Everything else is built from the present.
| Use | How | Example (to write)
| Past | past stem + ending | katabt (I wrote)
| Every day | b- + present | bekteb (I write)
| Right now | 3am + every-day form | 3am bekteb (I'm writing)
| Future | ra7 + present without b- | ra7 ekteb (I'll write)
| After badde, lezim, fiye | present without b- | badde ekteb (I want to write)
| Command | own form | ekteb! (write!)
## The prefixes of the "every day" form
| Person | Prefix | Example
| ana | b- | bekteb
| enta | bt- | btekteb
| ente | bt- … -e | btektebe
| huwwe | by- | byekteb
| hiyye | bt- | btekteb
| ne7na | mn- | mnekteb
| ento | bt- … -o | btektebo
| hinne | by- … -o | byektebo
Notice that "you (man)" and "she" are the same: btekteb. The context tells them apart.
## The endings of the past
| Person | Ending | Example
| ana / enta | -t | katabt
| ente | -te | katabte
| huwwe | (none) | katab
| hiyye | -et | katabet
| ne7na | -na | katabna
| ento | -to | katabto
| hinne | -o | katabo
ex: katabt risele | كتبت رسالة | I wrote a letter
ex: bekteb kel yom | بكتب كل يوم | I write every day
ex: 3am bekteb | عم بكتب | I'm writing
ex: ra7 ekteb bukra | رح اكتب بكرا | I'll write tomorrow
ex: kent ekteb | كنت اكتب | I used to write
## Object endings
"me, you, him" go on the end of the verb with the same endings as "my, your, his" (with -ne for "me").
ex: shefto | شفتو | I saw him
ex: shefta | شفتا | I saw her
ex: b7ebbak | بحبّك | I love you (to a man)
ex: b7ebbik | بحبِّك | I love you (to a woman)
ex: 3ayyetle | عيّطلي | Call me (to a man)

@@ commands | 📢 | verbs | Commands and "don't!"
> Telling someone to do something, or not to, politely.
## Commands
There are three forms: to a man, to a woman, to a group. The woman form ends in -e, the group in -o.
| To a man | To a woman | To a group | English
| ta3a | ta3e | ta3o | come
| rou7 | rou7e | rou7o | go
| kol | kele | kelo | eat
| khod | khede | khedo | take
| 2a3od | 2o3de | 2o3do | sit
| stanna | stanne | stanno | wait
| shouf | shoufe | shoufo | look
| es2al | es2ale | es2alo | ask
## Don't!
Use **ma** + the "you" form of the present without b-: btrou7 → ma trou7 (don't go).
ex: ma trou7! | ما تروح! | Don't go! (to a man)
ex: ma trou7e! | ما تروحي! | Don't go! (to a woman)
ex: ma t3assib | ما تعصّب | Don't get angry (to a man)
ex: ma tkhaf | ما تخاف | Don't be afraid (to a man)
## Making it polite
Add **please** (yes, the English word), **iza bitred** (if you like, to a man; "iza btride" to a woman), **min fadlak** (more formal) or start with **fik…?** (can you…?).
ex: stanna shway iza bitred | استنّى شوي إذا بتريد | Wait a moment, please (to a man)
ex: fik tsakker el beb? | فيك تسكّر الباب؟ | Could you close the door? (to a man)
ex: 3mol ma3rouf | اعمل معروف | Do me a favour / please (to a man)
ex: yalla, ta3o! | يلّا، تعو! | Come on, come (all of you)!
## Commands you'll hear every day
"tfaddal" (here you go / come in / help yourself), "khalas" (stop, done), "yalla" (come on, let's go), "shouf" (look), "2ol" (say: "2ol mar7aba" = say hello), "dakhlak" (please, I beg you).

?: "Don't go!" to a man: | mesh rou7 | *ma trou7 | la2 rou7
?: Which is the most polite? | rou7! | *fik trou7 iza bitred? | rou7 hala2!

## nshalla: the most misunderstood word
"bteje bukra?" "nshalla." This is "I hope so", not "yes". For a real yes you'll hear "akid" or "akid nshalla".
## 7abibi is not romantic
Shopkeepers, taxi drivers and grandmothers all say 7abibi. It's warm, not flirting. Men say it to men all the time.
tip: "yalla bye" is a full Lebanese goodbye. So is "yalla, bye, ciao, bonne journée".

@@ code-switching | 🇫🇷 | culture | French and English in Lebanese
> Why you'll hear "hi, kifak, ça va?" and how to join in.
## Three languages, one sentence
Most Lebanese learn Arabic, French and English at school, and mix them freely. "hi kifak ça va" is a real greeting. You can use English words too: it's normal, not a failure.
| Word | Language | Use
| merci | French | thanks (as common as shukran)
| bonjour / bonsoir | French | hello, good evening
| pardon | French | sorry, excuse me
| ça va? | French | how's it going?
| bonne journée | French | have a good day
| ok, sorry, please, bye | English | everywhere
| service | French | a shared taxi (servees)
| ascenseur | French | lift (also "elevator")
| frigidaire | French | fridge ("frigidaire" or "barred")
| chauffage | French | heating
ex: hi, kifak, ça va? | hi، كيفك، ça va؟ | Hi, how are you, all good?
ex: merci ktir | merci كتير | Thanks a lot
ex: pardon, fiye mor2? | pardon، فيي مرق؟ | Excuse me, can I pass?
ex: ana ktir busy lyom | أنا كتير busy اليوم | I'm really busy today
ex: ba3atelak el location | بعتتلك الـ location | I sent you the location
## Arabic grammar on foreign words
Foreign words take Arabic endings: "cancelna" (we cancelled), "btesharje?" (do you charge it?), "fiye a3mol park hon?" (can I park here?).
ex: cancelna el meeting | cancelنا الـ meeting | We cancelled the meeting
ex: el phone khelset batterio | الـ phone خلصت بطاريتو | The phone's battery died
## When to use Arabic anyway
Use Arabic words when you know them: people notice and smile. But don't stop the conversation hunting for a word. Say it in English and carry on.

@@ manners | 🤝 | culture | Manners: visits, food, coffee, respect
> What people expect when you visit, eat, greet and talk to elders.
## Greetings
Shake hands with everyone when you arrive and leave, and ask how they are. Friends of the same sex often kiss on the cheek, usually three times (right, left, right). Some religious people don't shake hands with the other sex: wait to see if a hand is offered.
Ask about the family: "kif el 3ayle?" (How's the family?), "kif el wled?" (How are the kids?).
## Visiting someone
Bring something: sweets, fruit or flowers. Take your shoes off if the hosts do. You'll be offered coffee, juice, fruit and sweets. Saying no once is polite; they'll insist; it's fine to accept the second time.
ex: ahla w sahla, tfaddalo | أهلا وسهلا، تفضّلو | Welcome, come in (to a group)
ex: ma badde ghalbkon | ما بدّي غلّبكن | I don't want to trouble you
ex: shu hal 7ake! | شو هالحكي! | What are you saying! (it's no trouble)
ex: beitkon 7elo ktir | بيتكن حلو كتير | Your home is really lovely
## At the table
Wait for the host to say "tfaddalo" or "sa7tein". You'll be served more than you can eat; leaving a little on the plate tells the host you're full. Praise the food, especially to the cook: "yeslamo ideiki" (bless your hands, to a woman).
ex: shbe3t, merci, ktir tayyeb | شبعت، merci، كتير طيّب | I'm full, thanks, it was delicious
ex: yeslamo ideiki | يسلمو إيديكي | Bless your hands (to the woman who cooked)
## Coffee
Turkish-style coffee ("ahwe") is served in small cups. "sada" (no sugar), "wasat" (medium), "7elwe" (sweet). At a funeral it's served bitter. Coffee at the end of a visit often means the visit is ending.
ex: ahwe wasat iza bitred | قهوة وسط إذا بتريد | Medium-sweet coffee, please
## Elders and names
Call older men "3ammo" (uncle) and older women "tante" or "khalto" (auntie), even strangers. Grandparents are "jeddo" and "teta". People are often called by their eldest child's name: "Abu Karim" (Karim's father), "Emm Karim" (Karim's mother).
## Topics to be careful with
Religion, politics and the war are sensitive. Listen more than you talk. Money is often discussed openly (rent, salaries, prices), so don't be surprised if someone asks what you pay.
tip: If you're invited "ta3a la 3enna", it's often real. Ask "akid?" and set a day.

@@ religion-phrases | 🕊️ | culture | Phrases with God in them
> You'll hear "allah" many times a day from people of every faith. Here's what it means.
## Everyday, not religious
Lebanon has many religions, and "allah" phrases are used by Christians and Muslims alike. Using them doesn't say anything about your own beliefs; it's simply polite Lebanese.
| Latin | Arabic | Meaning | When
| nshalla | إن شاء الله | God willing | plans, hopes
| mashalla | ما شاء الله | what God has willed | praising a child, a house, a success
| l 7amdella | الحمد لله | thank God | "How are you?" "Fine, l 7amdella."
| allah ma3ak | الله معك | God be with you | goodbye
| walla | والله | by God | really, I swear
| ya allah | يا الله | oh God | effort, impatience
| allah ykhallik | الله يخلّيك | God keep you | please (strong)
| allah y3inak | الله يعينك | God help you | sympathy for someone busy or struggling
| allah ysam7ak | الله يسامحك | God forgive you | mild "shame on you", often joking
| allah yer7amo | الله يرحمو | God have mercy on him | mentioning someone who died
ex: kifak? l 7amdella | كيفك؟ الحمد لله | How are you? Fine, thank God
ex: mashalla 3al wled | ما شاء الله عالولاد | What lovely children
ex: allah ykhallik, sa3edne | الله يخلّيك، ساعدني | Please, help me (to a man)
ex: allah y3inak | الله يعينك | God help you (to a man)
## mashalla protects
If you praise a baby, a car or a new house, add "mashalla". Praise without it can sound like envy to some people.

?: "How are you?" "Fine, …" | *l 7amdella | allah ma3ak | ya allah

@@ lebanese-vs-msa | 📰 | culture | Lebanese vs. Standard Arabic
> What changes between what people speak and what you hear on the news.
## Two kinds of Arabic
Every Arab country has its own spoken Arabic. Next to it there's **Standard Arabic** (fus7a): the language of the news, books, the Quran and official signs. Nobody speaks fus7a at home. Kifak teaches spoken Lebanese, which is what you need for life in Lebanon.
## The main differences
| Standard | Lebanese | English
| al-aan | hala2 | now
| madha? | shu? | what?
| ayna? | wein? | where?
| kayfa 7aluk? | kifak? | how are you?
| uriid | badde | I want
| laysa | mesh | not
| ladayya | 3ande | I have
| sawfa aktub | ra7 ekteb | I'll write
| qalb | 2alb | heart | q becomes 2
| thalatha | tlete | three | th becomes t
| dhahab | dahab | gold | dh becomes d
## Sounds that change
The letter ق (q) is usually said as 2 in the city: "2alb" (heart), "2ahwe" (coffee). Villagers in some areas keep the q. ث (th) becomes t or s, ذ (dh) becomes d or z.
## Will Lebanese help with other countries?
Yes. Syrians, Palestinians and Jordanians speak something close to Lebanese (together called "Levantine"), and they'll understand you easily. Egyptians and Gulf Arabs will understand you too, because they watch Lebanese TV and hear Lebanese songs.

@@ texting | 📱 | culture | Texting in Lebanese
> How people write Lebanese on WhatsApp, with the abbreviations.
## Latin letters with numbers
Most Lebanese text in Latin letters with numbers for the extra sounds, the same system used on this site. Some people write in Arabic script instead. You'll see both.
| Text | Means
| kifak? | how are you?
| shou? / shu? | what?
| 3am bshoufak | see you (lit. I'm seeing you)
| ok tayyeb | ok fine
| ma3leish | sorry, never mind
| 7abibi | dear
| yalla bye | bye
| lol / hhhh | laughing
| inshala / nshalla / nshallah | hopefully
| mrc / merci | thanks
| bas | but / only
## Spelling isn't fixed
"kifak", "keefak" and "kefak" are all the same word. "8" for gh, "5" for kh and "9" for heavy s appear in some people's messages. Don't worry about the "right" spelling; read it out loud and it'll make sense.
ex: wselt? | وصلت؟ | Did you arrive? (the classic WhatsApp check)
ex: ana bel tari2 | أنا بالطريق | I'm on my way
ex: 5 d2aye2 w bkoun 3endak | ٥ دقايق وبكون عندك | Five minutes and I'll be at yours
ex: ba3atlak voice | بعتلك voice | I sent you a voice note
## Voice notes
Lebanese send a lot of voice notes. They're excellent practice: ask a friend to send you short ones and listen twice.
